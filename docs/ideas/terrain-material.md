# Terrain material: geology, detail, grass, wetness and snow in one

The landscape bench (docs/bench/landscape) found that the ground cannot show everything the engine knows about it
at once. The generator gives geology (soil, bare rock, scree, alluvium; `get_geology_shader_code()`) and erosion
detail below the vertex spacing (`get_detail_shader_code()`), GrassField3D gives the thatch under the grass
(`get_ground_shader_code()`), and the weather and the snowpack give wetness and snow. Wetness and snow exist only
as `BaseMaterial3D` features (`wetness_enabled`, `snow_enabled`), generated into its shader and, under path
tracing, applied in the ray generation shader from material flags (`subsurface.wetness`, `subsurface.snow`). The
geology and detail code only fits a `ShaderMaterial`. So the bench draws the ground as a `StandardMaterial3D`
with vertex colours: snow and wetness work, the geology look is a flat tint and the detail normals are gone.
Every terrain in a real scene hits the same wall, Terrain3D's own shader included.

## How others do it

- **Frostbite, Andersson 2007, "Terrain Rendering in Frostbite Using Procedural Shader Splatting"** (SIGGRAPH
  course): the terrain shader is assembled from rules on height, slope and normal, evaluated per pixel; artists
  paint masks only where the rules are not enough. Layers blend in the shader, not in a baked texture.
- **Far Cry 5, Moore 2018, "Terrain Rendering in Far Cry 5"** (GDC): world-space procedural layer weights from
  height, slope and flow maps, with a virtual texture cache; cliffs use a separate world-aligned (triplanar)
  path so steep faces do not stretch.
- **The Witcher 3, Gollent 2014, "Landscape Creation and Rendering in REDengine 3"** (GDC): two layers per
  texel (background and overlay) chosen by slope, with a slope-based blend and vertical-face projection.
- **Height-based blending, Mishkinis 2013, "Advanced Terrain Texture Splatting"**: each layer's height map
  decides who wins near a transition (stones poke through sand), instead of a soft cross-fade that looks like
  paint.
- **Biplanar mapping, Quilez 2020**: two texture fetches instead of triplanar's three, picking the two major
  axes; enough for cliffs and much cheaper, which matters under path tracing where every hit shades.
- **Unreal Engine 5**: Landscape Layer Blend node (weight or height blend) plus material functions for snow and
  wetness that projects share; Runtime Virtual Texturing caches the blended result. Snow and wetness are not
  built in; they are material functions driven by Material Parameter Collections.
- **Unity HDRP**: TerrainLit shader with four to eight layers, height-based blend; snow and wetness are again
  project-side Shader Graph sub-graphs reading global properties.
- **Wet surfaces, Lagarde 2013** (already used by `wetness_enabled`, see material-wetness.md) and **snow cover,
  Niu and Yang 2007** with the per-facet rule (already used by `snow_enabled`, see snow.md): these are the lines
  to reuse, not rewrite.

The common shape: one terrain shader built from reusable functions (layers by rules, height blend, world-aligned
cliffs, then weather on top), the weather functions shared with every other material.

## What we build

1. **The weather lines as shader code any material can use.** `BaseMaterial3D.get_wetness_shader_code()` and
   `get_snow_shader_code()` return the global uniforms and two functions,
   `weather_wetness(world_pos, world_normal, porosity, metallic, inout albedo, inout roughness)` and
   `weather_snow(world_pos, geo_normal, detail_normal, amount, max_slope, inout albedo, inout roughness, inout normal)`,
   generated by the same C++ that writes them into `BaseMaterial3D`, so there is one source and the two never
   drift apart. Under path tracing a `ShaderMaterial` already runs its own fragment code in a custom hit group,
   so the same lines run there with no ray generation flags; the check below confirms the global maps are bound
   for custom hit shaders. This alone fixes Terrain3D (its shader override takes the snippets).
2. **`TerrainMaterial3D`**, a material that writes its own shader like `BaseMaterial3D` does, so nothing is
   pasted by hand:
   - layers (albedo, normal, roughness, height) for rock, scree, soil, alluvium, sand, plus an optional grass
     layer; weights from the generator's geology globals (`terrain_geology`) and the data map (slope, wetness
     index), with Mishkinis height blending;
   - biplanar mapping above a slope threshold, plain world XZ below it;
   - the generator's detail normal (`terrain_detail_normal`, footprint-faded);
   - GrassField3D's ground tint where grass grows;
   - then wetness and snow from step 1, in that order (snow over wet ground, as the snowpack does).
   Without a generator it falls back to slope and height rules, so it also serves hand-made terrain.
3. The landscape bench switches its ground to it and the GAP line becomes a check.

## Far ground: plant cover colour past far_start (11 Nov)

Distant terrain reads by its cover, not its soil: a forested slope at 2 km is dark green even where no plant is drawn.
Engines bake a world colour map for the far field (Frostbite's terrain colour map, Andersson 2007; Far Cry 5's world
colour map, McLaren 2018; UE's landscape macro variation). Here the ground colour itself already comes right at
distance (geology map plus each layer texture's lowest mip), so only the cover is new. Vegetation publishes
`terrain_cover_map` (RGBA8: sRGB mean cover colour from above with canopy shade, alpha the cover share) and
`terrain_cover_rect` (x, z, width, depth), 4 m texels, from the same colours `vegetation_far()` uses.
`far_blend_enabled` (off by default; the code is only compiled when on) blends, between `far_start` (300 m) and
`far_start + far_fade` (150 m), from the near result (grass tint, `vegetation_far()`) to the bare ground under the
cover colour by its share, so the far cover replaces `vegetation_far()` and nothing stacks. It sits after the layer
blend and detail normal and before ground deformation, wetness and snow, which still lie on it. Unset globals (rect
width 0) leave the ground as it is. Distance is from the camera to the shaded point, the same rule
`vegetation_far()` uses, also on path-traced bounce hits.

## Sky occlusion: raster ambient light only from the sky the hills leave open (12 Nov)

The fault: at dusk a slope under a higher ridge was lit red in raster and dark under path tracing. With the sun
switched off the raster frame barely changed (6.4 against 6.6), so the light was the sky's: raster ambient light
integrates the whole sky around the normal (the radiance map), including the low sunset glow that the far valley
wall hides. The path tracer traces the wall and the slope goes dark (2.6). Daytime valleys carry the same error,
weaker. Screen-space AO reaches a few metres; this is hundreds.

How others do it:

- Horizon mapping (Max 1988) and the sky view factor of a sloped surface under its terrain horizons (Dozier and
  Frew 1990), the standard in terrain radiation models; our climate bake already uses both (`terrain_climate.h`).
- Bent normals and a scalar occlusion (Landis 2002; Unreal's distance field AO for the sky light; Godot's
  `BENT_NORMAL_MAP`). Open worlds bake a top-down world AO from the heightfield.
- Precomputed visibility as spherical harmonics (Sloan, Kautz and Snyder 2002), with order 2 enough for irradiance
  (Ramamoorthi and Hanrahan 2001).

What we tried, measured at three ground points against the exact integral of the real sunset panorama:

| method | left slope | right slope | floor |
|---|---|---|---|
| exact (the panorama, the horizons, the cosine) | 0.59 | 0.66 | 0.82 |
| sky view factor + bent normal (v1) | 0.91 | 0.76 | 0.84 |
| first-order harmonics on the pixel normal | 0.61 | 0.60 | 0.68 |
| second-order harmonics (built) | 0.58 | 0.67 | 0.80 |

A ratio per 8 m cell weighted by the sky's light (v2) rendered barely darker: the cell's normal is not the pixel's.
A view factor treats the sky as even; at dusk nearly all of it is a band a few degrees high in the west, exactly
what a ridge hides, so the occlusion must be weighed by the sky's own light, and per pixel normal (the 8 m cell's
normal can face the other way from a gully side).

What we build:

- `TerrainGenerator3D.sky_occlusion` (default on), after `generate()`: the final ground on a grid `sky_cell` (8 m)
  apart, horizons in 16 directions out to `sky_distance` (4 km, steps growing 8%, past the result the ground counts
  as level), kept per node (16 bytes). About 0.8 s for the 4 km valley.
- The sky's light: `sky_light_source` (a WorldEnvironment or Camera3D) is read back as a 64x32 radiance panorama
  (`RenderingServer.sky_bake_panorama`) every `sky_light_interval` (2 s), and only when it has changed by more
  than 2%. It is gathered into 16 x 16 direction bins; each node's seen sky (bins above its horizons, the horizon's
  bin in part) goes into nine harmonics of luminance, over the whole sky's first. About 90 ms on worker threads for
  513 x 513 nodes; `update_sky_light(panorama)` does the same by hand. Before any panorama, an even sky.
- Three layers of RGBA half (`terrain_sky_map`, a sampler2DArray) and the whole sky's harmonics
  (`terrain_sky_full`, `terrain_sky_full2`, `terrain_sky_full3`) as globals.
- `TerrainMaterial3D.sky_occlusion_enabled` (default on), raster only (`#if !defined(RT)`): `AO` = irradiance of the
  seen sky over that of the whole sky, both on the shading normal (so the detail normal keeps its shading),
  `AO_LIGHT_AFFECT` 0 (direct light untouched), then the multi-bounce fit of Jimenez et al. 2016 (GTAO) on the
  ground's albedo, since the hidden sky is replaced by lit ground, not black. `sky_occlusion` scales it.
  Small-scale occlusion stays with SSAO.

Result (valley view, mean pixel value, raster without / with / path traced): dusk ground 1.42 / 1.00 / 1.07, the
slope under the west wall 2.60 / 1.82 / 1.06; afternoon ground 24.4 / 22.2 / 22.8, a shaded slope 16.4 / 11.9 /
13.6 (raster has no bounce light from the sunlit wall opposite).

Cost: three bilinear fetches and two nine-term sums per ground pixel; the readback is a 64x32 copy every few
seconds while the sky changes. GPU timings on the PC before merge are owed.

## Cost and quality

- Raster: biplanar doubles fetches only on steep pixels; five layers with height blend is about what Terrain3D
  already pays. Expect a small cost against the vertex-colour stand-in and a large gain (real surfaces, detail
  normals under 4 m). Measured on the PC before merge.
- Path tracing: the custom hit group shades every terrain hit with the full layer stack; bounces see the same
  ground, so the detail is not lost under path tracing (the project rule). The cost is one heavier hit shader;
  measure with `--gpu-profile` on the PC.
- No visual loss for existing scenes: nothing changes unless a material uses the new code.

## Sand glitter (19 Nov, Paulo picked it from the desert research)

Dry quartz sand glints: a share of the grains (clean crystal and cleavage faces, mica) are near-mirror facets that
reflect the sun as points far brighter than the sand. Journey drew it with a random per-pixel grain normal and a
threshold (Edwards, GDC 2013, "Sand Rendering in Journey"; Zucconi's 2019 breakdown); our snow already counts facets
per pixel (snow_glint: Zirr and Kaplanyan 2016; Deliot and Belcour 2023).

- Grains 0.5 mm, a quarter of their area facets (cleavage and crystal faces, several to a grain) with GGX alpha 0.4
  normals. Each facet catches the sun over about 1.6 degrees (the sun's 0.53 degree disc and slight curvature),
  6e-4 sr. (First 10% and 3 degrees, 2.1e-3 sr: each flash came out 30 times dimmer than a flat face mirroring the
  sun and docs saw almost none into the mirror direction at 640 wide.) A flash's pixel brightness still scales with
  (grain / footprint)^2: glitter is a close-up and high-resolution effect, as in life.
- Path tracer: the same per-footprint count from the ray cone (RAY_FOOTPRINT, back to the pixel's width), each
  sample taking the facet lobe with its share; a per-grain draw per sample had converged to speckle.
- Raster: the count of facets in the pixel mirroring the sun is Poisson with mean facets x D(h) cos x
  omega / (4 VoH), drawn per footprint cell so a glint stays on its grains while sun and camera hold still. Each
  adds (grain / footprint)^2 of the pixel at the sun's irradiance over omega, drawn as the coat lobe turned onto
  the half vector (alpha 0.011), so the sun's shadow and colour apply. Glints thin out with distance (a pixel's
  facets are a smaller share of it) instead of turning to noise.
- Path tracer: every sample lands on one grain; a facet grain puts the base specular on its own normal
  (SPECULAR_NORMAL) at roughness 0.12, the rest keep the sand's. The tracer integrates the footprint itself.
- Off under rain (wetness 0.05-0.3), on the soaked swash, under grain cover and under snow.

## Far sun shadows (19 Nov, item 4 of the round)

At a low sun the shadow cascades smear and then end; far ridges cast nothing. Height field self-shadowing
(Timonen and Westerholm 2010, "Scalable height field self-shadowing"; horizon mapping, Max 1988) gives exact
terrain shadows at any range from the ground's horizon toward the sun.

- TerrainGenerator3D keeps the sky grid's heights (sky_cell, 8 m) and, when the sun's azimuth moves a quarter
  degree, sweeps each line of a grid turned to the sun with the upper convex hull of the ground already passed
  (amortised constant time a point), giving each point's horizon tangent toward the sun. The map does not depend
  on the elevation, so the whole day costs a rebuild every quarter degree of azimuth.
- Shader globals terrain_sun_horizon_map / _frame / _size. TerrainMaterial3D shades SUN_SHADOW by
  smoothstep(tangent - 0.006, tangent + 0.014, tan(sun elevation)), fading in from far_shadow_start (150 m) to
  twice it, so the cascades keep the near ground. Raster only; the path tracer traces the hills.
- The sun is sun_light, or the first visible DirectionalLight3D (polled five times a second).
- Each node also keeps one over the run to its hull point and its own grid height, so the material moves the
  tangent to the pixel's own height: slope + (grid height - pixel height) / run. Crests standing above the 8 m
  grid stay lit and hollows under it shade, as the cascades show them.
- Check (19 Nov, docs asked whether it darkens the ambient): it scales SUN_SHADOW only, the sun's direct light. With
  the default 6 km cascades the frame with far shadows on matches the frame with them off to within 0.3 of a level
  in mean brightness; the dark range in the first after frame was right for a sun 2 degrees up (the before frame,
  cascades cut at 300 m, lit what the ridges shade).


## Flat layers (20 Nov)

Docs found a flat olive river bank (scene) and a flat green hill (rocks). Both were ground layers with no textures
(the benches set none: alluvium's default is olive, soil's a dark olive green), drawn as one colour. A layer without
an albedo texture now gets value-noise tone over 9 m and 2.2 m and, while the pixel is finer than them, 0.25 m clods
and 0.06 m grit as tone and a light bump (forward differences), each faded out as the footprint nears it. The far

them; this is the floor for any layer that has none.

Built. Scans in art-src terrain/layers (2K JPG, Poly Haven CC0, CREDIT.txt): rock dark_rock_02 (2.0 m), scree
rocks_ground_02 (2.0 m), soil forest_ground_06 (2.11 m), alluvium gravelly_sand (2.48 m), sand sand_03 (2.0 m). The
benches load them from docs/bench/landscape/art/terrain_layers (fetch_art.sh; .gdignore'd, read with
Image.load_from_file); --ground-scans=0 for plain layers, --ground-scans=rock,scree for some, --scan-maps=anrh.
- TerrainMaterialLayer.detail_only (on): the albedo and roughness maps are divided by their own means, so a scan adds
  detail and the layer keeps the geology's colour and roughness. Poly Haven roughness maps follow no common scale
  (brown_mud_03 averages 0.52, rock_face_04 0.96): taken as they are, a wet-mud scan made dry soil glossy.
- Picking scans: low contrast around the mean. rock_face_04 (bright leaf litter on dark rock, p98/mean 2.7) put the
  rock at half the layer colour once normalised; dark_rock_02 (cv 0.22) holds it.
- The 9 m / 2.2 m tone now also runs on textured layers (sand included): hex tiling hides seams, not sameness.
- Godot takes a sampler argument only with one hint per function: tm_hex is written once per map kind (a, n, r, h);
  textured layers had never compiled.
- tm_hex bounds the footprint to half a tile (faces seen edge on stretch the cone up to 20 times).
- Cliff biplanar weights: the sum was floored at 1e-4, so on faces whose normal points near a cube diagonal (both
  weights about 1e-5) the textures came out at a tenth: black patches on cliffs. Normalised exactly now.
- Crack lips on rock faces capped at some 34 degrees (depth 0.45 of a lip 2 pixels or 6 cm wide): the 56 to 76
  degree lips drew open joints as black lines on sunlit sea cliffs.

- Erosion detail normal (TerrainGenerator3D terrain_detail_normal): the octaves below the vertex spacing keep the
  geometry's depth ratio (0.08), so each adds a slope near 0.5 and four of them tilted gentle ground 30 to 50
  degrees. Under a 32 degree sun those faces turned dark: blotches that read as wet ground, raster and PT alike,
  with or without scans (gone with --detail-octaves=0). The added slope now saturates softly near 0.35 (about 20
  degrees, small rill walls in soil).
- The erosion detail took its gullies' direction and slope mask from the already bumped normal (procedural clods or
  a scan's normal map), so they turned every few centimetres: blobs with hard rims, a melted-wax look over bare
  ground without scans. It now runs on the surface's own normal (cliff relief included) and its tilt goes on top
  of the bump. Its slope also had hard switches (normalising by max(len, 0.5), abs(s) and the sign of s at the
  creases): fine in the geometry, hard-edged patches in per-pixel light. They are rounded (smooth maximum over
  0.3, creases over 0.15).
- No-scan ground, round two (docs: pale islands with dark rims, smear, diagonal ripple). The value noise hashed
  with sin(), which loses precision once its argument reaches the thousands (fine octaves a few hundred metres out):
  an integer hash now. The procedural floor had two bump octaves (0.25 m, 6 cm) on one square lattice: five now,
  0.6 m to 1 cm, each turned against the last. The erosion detail drops its gullies under about 2 m (smoothstep
  1.5 to 4 m): drawn in the light alone they were ripples and rimmed islands; the layers carry that scale.
- Rock and soil met along smooth slope and geology contours (a cut-out olive edge on grey): the slope thresholds and
  the geology's rock share are pushed by noise (5 m, and 1.3 m faded with the footprint), so outcrops break into
  tongues and islands. Under cliff relief the layers go by the heightfield's normal (CUSTOM1), not the displaced
  faces': each ledge's flat tread took the soil, olive ladder strips under the lips. The path tracer still draws
  those strips (it reads the displaced normal); the lips' undersides read as thin black lines in raster only.
- Pale rock slopes and faces read flat from afar, with only the fine relief (scan or procedural bump) on an even
  colour: crumpled paper. Past about 32 degrees the rock layer now takes streaks down the fall line (1.3 m by 14 m,
  4 m by 30 m), bed bands (0.9 m) and 18 m tone, and its fine relief eased by up to 0.4.

## Rock faces weather by aspect: research (22 Nov, card posted)

Today faces carry random streaks and bands (6c23dd82a0) that ignore where water and sun actually go.
- What faces show: crustose and foliose lichen and mosses cover far more of shaded, damp aspects (north in the
  northern hemisphere) and less of sunny, dry ones (Armstrong 1974 and 2011 on lichen growth and aspect; Pentecost
  1980). Below ledges, joints and overhangs water seeps leave dark drip lines: cyanobacterial ink streaks on
  limestone ("Tintenstriche", Gloeocapsa and Scytonema crusts; Viles 1995, Danin 1983) and, in arid climates, desert
  varnish, a dark Mn-Fe coating that follows the same runoff lines (Dorn and Oberlander 1981). Ledge tops below bird
  perches take orange nitrophilous lichen (Xanthoria) on coasts. Fresh scars from rockfall are paler and bare.
- How others do it: the classic offline model is flow-based weathering, water particles running down the surface
  depositing and dissolving stain (Dorsey, Pedersen and Hanrahan 1996, "Flow and changes in appearance"; Chen et al.
  2005, gamma-ton tracing). Games bake or approximate it: world-aligned masks by normal, curvature and occlusion
  (Horizon Zero Dawn and RDR2 cliff layering; UE's material layering with world-normal and AO-driven grime;
  Megascans' "drip" masks) and drip streaks as fall-line-stretched noise starting under convex edges.
- Plan: (1) aspect and wetness: shade from the sun's mean path (latitude from the weather map, the face's
  aspect and slope) times moisture (WeatherMap3D precipitation and the sky occlusion map) drives lichen cover on
  rock and scree (grey-green crusts and the occasional yellow-green map lichen, as the scree stones already use),
  moss on the dampest and least steep shaded faces. (2) seep streaks from the cliff relief: the bed field
  (tm_cl_beds, the same one the vertices use) gives each proud bed's lower lip; under it a dark streak runs down the
  fall line, fading over 2 to 8 m, broken across the face by drip-line noise, stronger where the face is wetter and
  on limestone (geology). (3) fresh scars: live rockfall events and the stand's disturbance age keep a pale bare
  patch that greys over years. CPU mirror for WorldGround albedo; raster and PT frames of a north and a south face.

## Frost-sorted ground in the material (22 Nov, ground detail's follow-up)

RockField3D's frost sorting placed the border stones but left the fine centres in the meadow's sward. Sorted circles'
centres are frost boils: silt and clay heaved every winter, so no sward holds, wet through the thaw, darker than the
stones (Washburn 1956; Walker et al. 2004, frost-boil ecosystems; Kessler and Werner 2003).
- RockField3D publishes terrain_frost_map (RG8: gate, stone share, the same functions its stones are placed by) over
  terrain_frost_rect, 64 m round the target at 0.125 m, on a worker job per 8 m step (gate on a 2 m grid, share per
  texel where open; the stripe blocks' fall lines cached).
- TerrainMaterial3D: centres (share under about 0.65) take a grey-brown silt, about 0.15 dry in this material's linear
  albedo and 0.6 of that wet, drying in patches, matte (damp, not puddled); borders a faint gravel lag. Transitions
  span the share's range, so a mossy rim stays where the fines meet the stones. In the cold bench climate the weather's
  hoarfrost lies over the wet silt (pale centres at dawn).
- Rocks bench: --no-frost-ground for the before frame (stones kept). Images /mnt/project-files/fork/terrain/frost/.

## Relief on moderate slopes (22 Nov, Paulo's pick)

Built: Landscape3D.bank_relief (0.8 m; 0.4 read too faint at 25 m) with _bank_offset and _relief_weights (C++) and
tm_bk_offset / tm_cl_disp in the material (pixel normal, ground patch, ledge shadow march; CUSTOM1.w carries the bank
scale). Landscape3D now answers WorldGround.relief_at (WorldReliefProvider, d tan(slope) at a fixed xz, full field)
and publishes terrain_relief_map (RG16F, 128 m at 0.5 m round the camera, rebuilt per 16 m step or height change).
Rocks bench: --bank-relief=x, --bank-spot (a 42-52 degree soil bank, raking view along it). Images in
/mnt/project-files/fork/terrain/banks/ (raster and PT pairs agree). Plan as researched:

Banks and cave mouths at 35 to 50 degrees draw as plain 4 m heightfield triangles with long straight silhouettes:
cliff relief (Landscape3D::_cliff_offset) only starts at 50 degrees. Soil banks do not keep beds and joints; they slump.
- What real banks look like: shallow translational slides and slumps leave scarps 0.3 to 1.5 m high and lobes and
  hummocks below them over 3 to 15 m (Varnes 1978 and Cruden and Varnes 1996, landslide types; Highland and
  Bobrowsky 2008, USGS landslide handbook); grazed and frost-crept grassland at 30 to 45 degrees carries terracettes,
  steps 0.2 to 0.5 m tall and 0.3 to 1 m apart along the contour (Vincent and Clarke 1976; Bielecki and Mueller 2002),
  too fine for vertices, so those go to the per-pixel normal only.
- How engines do it: the same as cliffs, displacement along the heightfield normal on the finest chunks with a
  per-pixel normal from the same analytic field (REDengine 3, Gollent 2014; Frostbite, Widmark 2012; Horizon
  Zero Dawn's terrain, van Muijden 2017, keeps the displacement field shared with placement so foliage sits on it).
- Plan: a bank offset in Landscape3D (C++ and the material's GLSL port, as for the beds): slump scarps along
  the contour from a 1D noise in the fall line's coordinate with lobes below, amplitude about 0.35 of the cliff
  relief, weight rising from 35 to 45 degrees and handing over to the beds by 55. CUSTOM1.w (free) carries the bank
  share so the pixel normal and ledge shadow march know which field to use. Terracettes in the normal only.
- Contract gap: vegetation, rocks, ground detail and collision sit on the heightfield, not on the drawn relief.
  Cliffs carry few plants, banks carry many, so a 0.2 m relief would float or bury grass and stems. So: (1) bank
  relief weighted by bare share (the material's soil exposure and the generator's cover: bare banks, scree, cave
  mouths, gully sides) until (2) a published drawn-ground contract, Landscape3D.get_cliff_point (exists, used by
  waterfalls) or a height-offset map like terrain_stand_map, which vegetation, rocks and ground detail read.
  (2) is a world-links item for the coordinator.
- Check: raster and PT frames of a cave mouth bank and a bare gully side, before/after (Landscape3D bank_relief 0),
  plants on the bank not floating, triangle count on the finest chunks.

## Wet ground glints seen edge on (22 Nov, specular antialiasing tried)

Rocks found single bright pixels down the lit sea cave's mouth (the cave's ground, drawn with this material, wet,
seen edge on against a low sun ahead), which TAA turns into a pale hairline. Asked for specular antialiasing.
- Tried: geometric specular AA (Kaplanyan et al. 2016; Tokuyoshi and Kaplanyan 2019 and 2021; HDRP's
  GeometricNormalFiltering, UE's r.NormalCurvatureToRoughness): alpha^2 += min(variance / (2 pi), 0.18) from the
  shading normal's screen derivatives, on this material and the cave walls. No change on the dots, even with the
  kernel at its clamp (roughness 0.3 to about 0.66). Then Smith masking on the geometric normal (Schuessler et al.
  2017) for bent shading normals: no change either.
- Debug render of those pixels: both the geometric and the shading normal face the eye by under 0.1, wetness puts
  roughness under 0.4, and the sun 12 degrees up ahead sits near the mirror direction. Turning specular off clears
  46 of 52 dots. So the normal does not vary inside the pixel; it is the true grazing glint of a wet surface (GGX
  with Fresnel near 1 at grazing), on a strip under a pixel wide. That is geometric (coverage) aliasing, not
  specular aliasing: no NDF filter sees it, and TAA's faint line is the coverage-weighted answer.
- Godot's own limiter (scene_forward_clustered.glsl) adds its kernel to alpha instead of alpha^2, under-filtering
  glossy surfaces; left as is (changing it shifts every material, ocean glitter among them).
- Nothing kept in code (no shown gain). Open: a PT frame of the view to confirm the line is real (60 min cloud
  timeout at 960x540, 64 spp, so PC queue); if PT shows it, it stays. If it should not be lit at all, the question
  is the strip's shadowing or wetness at the mouth (rocks).

## Rills and gullies (22 Nov, Paulo's pick)

The generator already cuts rills and gullies: tile erosion (droplets on a 2 m grid, terrain-generation.md stage 4) is
on by default, but both benches switched it off for speed, so no frame showed it. The rocks bench now runs it by
default (--no-tiles for a before frame). Built on top:
- Channels. The droplets' flow is published as terrain_tile_flow_map (R16F flow, G16F deposit m) with
  terrain_tile_flow_rect; terrain_gully(p, ny) in the generator's shader code and TerrainGenerator3D.get_gully_at
  give the channel floor share. Rule: the slope-area index A S^2 (Montgomery and Dietrich 1988, 1992) from 300 to
  1200 m2, times the flow's concentration against its mean 6 m out (1.5 to 3 times). The papers' 25 to 250 m2 marked
  nearly every mountain flank: the droplets' flow overstates the contributing area on steep ground. The
  concentration term keeps only the thalwegs (on the rocks world, 2 to 5 m wide lines down each gully).
- Geometry. gully_incision (1) cuts each channel deeper and narrower than the 2 m droplets can: depth 0.05 sqrt(A)
  m up to 2 m (half in rock; gully depth grows with stream power, about A^0.5 S: Poesen et al. 2003, Kirkby and
  Bracken 2009), shaped across the line by the channel share. In get_height(), so collision, the landscape, the ground
  patch, rocks and plants all see it. Rocks bench: --gully-incision=0 for a before frame, --gully-spot to look at one.
- Material. Channel floors in soil become a gravel lag (scree layer: the flow strips the fines, Poesen et al. 2003),
  lose the sward tint and read damp (albedo 0.78, Lobell and Asner 2002); deposits over 0.15 m on gentle ground
  read as fresh fines (alluvium layer). TerrainMaterial3D's CPU ground contract mirrors both.
- (24 Nov) Fines settle only where the gradient falls off: the fan rule takes alluvium from 18 degrees down (full by 10),
  the floodplain rule from 23 (full by 14), the rim broken by the edge noise. Fluvial fans are mostly under 5 degrees and
  debris-flow fans reach about 15 (Blair and McPherson 1994); taken to 32 degrees, 25 degree dry slopes drew flat pale
  slabs of bare alluvium with hard edges.
- Open: on the rocks world tile erosion's 15 m max depth makes canyons of most soil gullies; sub-2 m rills still only
  in the erosion detail normal.

## Trails and rooting (21 Nov)

WorldStand's worn trails (trail_at: passes a year, tread share) and boar rooting (rooting_age_at) now mark the
ground layers. Landscape3D rasterises both round its camera into globals terrain_stand_map (RG8, 512 texels over
256 m) and terrain_stand_rect, rebuilt when the camera crosses a 64 m step or the stand's generation changes:
- r: tread share times the wear the passes give, 1 - exp(-passes / 300) (trampling takes a field layer's cover off
  roughly so: Cole 1995, Liddle 1997; a few hundred passes a year keep a line bare). The trails' segments are copied
  and drawn per texel; a script stand is sampled per metre through trail_at.
- g: freshness of rooted soil, 1 to a season after the boar, 0 by two years (litter and the field layer close over
  it; Bueno 2013, Welander 2000), from rooting_age_at every 2 m, bilinear.
The material draws trodden soil as the sward's own paths do (grass_path_soil): compacted, darker and browner, damp in
the hollows, smoother, the bump pressed flat; the tread's edge fairly sharp and ragged at two scales. Rooting is dark
moist humus in fist-sized clods (bump) with overturned sods among them, in patches with ragged edges, browning and
greying toward the bare soil as it ages. Neither on rock, sand or slopes past about 40 degrees. Stones on trails come
from ground detail, litter from vegetation. Rocks bench: --stand-test [--stand-at=x,z] [--rooting-age=y].
Check: raster (TAA) and PT agree at the bench frame; PT keeps the clods and the tread's edge.
Round 2 (docs review): the tread now has banks (a normal from the wear map's slope, about 8 cm deep), a darker band of
crumbled soil under the turf's broken lip, hoof pocking about 1 cm deep and bared grit while a pixel resolves it.
The dark ovals in that frame were not the trail: the scree's parallax stones cast their sun shadow test into soil
where the scree weight thins to a few per cent (stones millimetres tall, but a penumbra and bias that do not shrink
with them). tm_rd_pom_shadow now fades with the stones' height.

## Bare soil from the climate (24 Nov, gap from ground detail)

The soil and alluvium layers' colours are a vegetated topsoil (olive), right under a closed sward and wrong in a dry
climate: at 350 mm a year about 70% of the ground is bare. Research: perennial plant cover on soil follows the year's
rain, about a tenth of the ground at 200 mm to most of it by 700 mm (rain-use curves of semi-arid rangelands, Le
Houerou 1984), and dryland cover is patchy, not thin: clumps with bare soil between, patches of metres (self-organised
patchiness, Rietkerk et al. 2004). Bare dry soil colours from the Munsell soil charts: a brown loam 10YR 5/3, a
grey-brown silt 10YR 6/2.

- One rule: `WorldContracts::bare_share_for_precipitation(mm)` (0.9 at 200 mm to 0 at 700 mm) and
  `climate_bare_share_at(p)` (the weather's season climate times the climate's factor there). The stones' rain
  splash pedestals read the same share (RockField3D keeps its own copy until the rocks work switches to this).
- Shader: precipitation from TerrainDataMap3D's `terrain_climate_map` (g, m a year) where it reaches, else the
  weather's new `weather_climate` global (x mm a year, w 1 while set; WeatherMap3D publishes it, zeroed when it
  leaves). No climate: the layers' colours as before, so humid worlds are unchanged.
- Soil and alluvium break into bare patches and cover by the share: a two-octave noise (4 m, 1.3 m) under the share's
  quantile (logistic probit; measured mean 0.5, sd 0.16), so the bare area is the share; past a footprint of about half
  a metre the patches fade to the share. Bare patches take `bare_soil_color` / `bare_alluvium_color` with the scans'
  detail kept. Grass fields and far plant cover still draw over it.
- CPU: `contract_albedo_at` mixes the same share (the patches' mean). Setting: `TerrainMaterial3D.climate_bare_soil`.
- (24 Nov) Snow on the bare patches: a thin fresh pack settles on the flattest facets of the detail normal first, and
  the soil scan's normal drew bare ground as rough, so a 15 m bare patch stayed brown under 1.6 cm of fresh snow while
  the turf round it was white. Bare soil's relief is millimetres to a couple of centimetres, buried by the first
  centimetre or two, so the snow's hold takes a normal flattened toward the surface's by the bare share.
- (25 Nov) Snow on gentle bedrock: the same rule read the walls of the rock scan's joints (and its macro variation's,
  about a metre across) as steep facets and left them bare, black slivers through 2 cm of snow on the scree scout's
  rock flat. A fresh pack fills a slab's joints and pits before it covers its knobs (snow smooths the ground it lands
  on, depressions first: Veitinger et al. 2014), so rock below about 23 degrees takes the bare patches' flattened
  normal by its share; faces keep the rule and their broken patches.

### Cured cover in summer-dry climates (24 Nov, the bare-soil scout read green)

The scout at 320 mm and a 26 C July still read green between the bare patches: the cover between them took the soil
layer's olive, a humid sward. The aridity there (P/PET 0.32) is right: semi-arid on the UNEP scale (0.2 to 0.5),
De Martonne 320 / (17 + 10) = 12, also semi-arid. Where summers are dry, grasslands cure: annual and perennial grasses
senesce in the drought and stand as straw until the rains (grassland curing, as fire danger rating tracks it); under
about 500 mm a year they cure fully, humid ones over about 1000 mm stay green.
- One rule: `WorldContracts::cured_share_for_precipitation(mm)` (1 at 500 mm, 0 from 1000 mm) and
  `climate_cured_share_at(p)`; GLSL `wc_climate_cured(p)` from the same precipitation (`wc_climate_mm`).
- The soil's cover takes `cured_cover_color` (senescent grassland, Munsell 2.5Y 5/4) by the share, the alluvium's by
  0.8 of it; bare patches still take the bare colours. Same on the CPU (`contract_albedo_at`).
- Seasonal (24 Nov, second pass): the share now follows the weather's season, as the sward's own greenness does: one
  rule, `WorldContracts::greenness_for_season(soil_moisture, temperature)` (PhenoGrass, Hufkens et al. 2016; the Growing
  Season Index of Jolly et al. 2005: green with soil water over about 40% of capacity and days above freezing), and
  `cured_share_for_season` = 1 - it. `climate_cured_share_at(p)` reads WorldWeather.season_at(p); shaders read
  `weather_climate` y (soil moisture) and z (the day's mean temperature), which WeatherMap3D now publishes, set again
  when the day moves the season. A Mediterranean or semi-arid cover greens in the wet season and cures in the summer
  drought; a humid one stays green, a hard winter leaves it tan. Without a weather: the precipitation rule above.
  Vegetation's GrassField3D.season_greenness is the same formula and can call the contract.
- Texture (docs review: flat mustard blobs): the cover is standing straw, grey weathered litter and soil between the
  tussocks (about a third), at 0.35 m and 0.1 m with 1.4 m patches of greyer and fresher, each scale fading to its
  mean once a pixel spans a quarter of it (faded at a pixel's own size they aliased into smooth bands under the
  temporal filter); `cured_cover_color` is their mean. Each tussock is lit on top and shaded at its base (0.62 to
  1.3), and clumps and swales of 3 m vary the cover's tone and how much soil shows (0.72 to 1.28), what a dry sward
  shows from afar: with colour alone (plus or minus 14%) the second review still read flat blobs.

## Terracette steps (24 Nov)

Grazed slopes of 25 to 51 degrees carry terracettes: stock walk across the slope and their paths settle into steps a
few metres apart, a level tread and a riser grazed between (Seleb, Chatterjee and Bhamla 2025). Vegetation's sward
draws them from the shared layout (`WorldContracts::terracette_at`: tread, phase up the step, strength); the ground now
builds them as relief.
- Profile in units of the step's rise (`TERRACETTE_RISE`, 2 m of height: 3.4 m apart along a 36 degree slope): flat
  over the tread (the first `TERRACETTE_TREAD`, 0.3, of the phase, where the layout's tread ends), the riser taking the
  rest, less its mean, so the ground keeps its height on average. Steps of about 0.6 m, treads of about a metre,
  risers some 10 degrees steeper than the slope, times the layout's strength at the step. At a tread of 0.185 the
  risers stood only 4 degrees steeper and the docs review saw one faint step.
- The step is the shared contract's (`WorldContracts::terracette_step`, GLSL `wc_terracette_step`, gate
  `wc_terracette_soil`), so plants and stones add it at their own roots: the GPU relief map leaves it out (sampled at
  its texels a 3 m pattern aliases; blades stood up to 0.37 m off the drawn ground). `WorldGround.relief_at` keeps it.
- The layout's phase goes by height over that fixed rise. It first took the rise from the local slope (3.4 m times its
  sine), and 100 m up a few centimetres' change of rise moved the phase by a whole step: the bands broke into a
  scribble of short dashes wherever the slope varied (the sward's too). A fixed rise keeps each band on one contour.
- Filtered, not sampled: the profile's integral is closed form, so each reader takes its box-filtered mean over its own
  grid (prefiltering, as for any procedural pattern): the landscape's vertices over twice the vertex spacing (wider
  with distance; at one spacing a metre grid drew each step with two or three vertices, a sawtooth along the
  contours), the near patch over twice its grid, the pixel's slope over its footprint. A grid coarser than a tread gets the
  steps' mean, never an alias of them.
- Geometry in both renderers: TerrainMaterial3D's vertex stage moves the landscape and the near patch; the per-pixel
  slope sharpens the tread edges and fades out where a pixel covers a step. Placers read it through
  WorldGround.relief_at (Landscape3D adds it, filtered over 0.5 m).
- On open soil only: none on sand or thin soil, nor in a geology cell with any rock, talus or till, where the layer
  mix lays a steep slope bare (the first test spots were steps on grey debris).
- Setting: `Landscape3D.terracette_relief` (share, 1), published in `terrain_bank_relief.y` for the material.

## Next round candidates (21 Nov, card posted)

### Rills and gullies as geometry (recommended)
Bare and thinly covered slopes in the frames are smooth between the generator's 4 m vertices; real hillslopes carry
rills (cm deep, 0.2-1 m apart, parallel down the fall line) and gullies (0.5-3 m deep) wherever overland flow
concentrates. Established approach:
- Channel initiation by a slope-area threshold: a channel starts where S * A^2 (Montgomery & Dietrich 1988, 1992;
  also tan(S) * sqrt(A) forms) exceeds a threshold set by cover and soil; vegetated ground needs much larger areas
  than bare regolith. The generator already has D8 drainage (drainage, channel_area) and geology (soil depth, cover).
- Spacing and depth: rill spacing from the hillslope length to the first channel (Horton's belt of no erosion),
  depth growing with stream power (k A^m S^n, the generator's area_exponent / flow_exponent), capped by soil depth on
  soil and by joints on rock.
- Geometry, not a texture: as cliff relief does, displace Landscape3D's finest chunks along the heightfield normal
  by a channel profile (V in regolith, U/box in cohesive soil, after Poesen et al. 2003, gully erosion review), with
  CUSTOM data so the material's per-pixel normal resolves the banks beyond the vertex spacing. Hydrology-based
  procedural terrain (Genevaux et al. 2013, "Terrain generation using procedural models based on hydrology") and
  Cordonnier et al. 2023 (interactive erosion authoring) both seed channels from drainage, as above.
- Material: channel floors get wash (fines, gravel lag), banks expose subsoil (paler, the soil colour of depth), and
  the wetness map runs wetter along floors. Ground detail and vegetation read a published channel mask (same global
  pattern as terrain_stand_map) for gravel lag and riparian plants.
- Check: raster and PT frames of a bare talus apron and a meadow slope; no visible repetition (spacing jittered per
  catchment); cost of the extra finest-chunk vertices on the cliff-relief path.

### Relief on moderate slopes
- (25 Nov) Lichen colonies as mosaics: thalli of 5 to 11 cm in 15 cm cells touch and fuse inside a colony (Armstrong
  2011 on crustose colonies), and from afar a colony averages to its crowded cover (0.55 of the density). Cells of
  0.4 m with mostly 3 to 6 cm thalli scattered lone round dots evenly 40 cm apart, a regular pattern of white spots
  on the cliff (docs review of the ledge frames); smaller thalli in the small cells only turned them into speckle. The
  map lichen a dull yellow-green (about 5Y 6/6), not a bright fleck.
Cliff relief starts at 50 degrees (full by 62). Banks and cave mouths at 35-50 degrees stay as flat 4 m triangles with
long straight silhouette edges. Extend the face weight down to about 35 degrees with a smaller amplitude (a soil bank
slumps, it does not keep joints), using the same _cliff_offset noise scaled by slope. Watch the triangle budget and
the PT triangle split Valence is chasing (it lives on the relief chunks).

### Rock faces weather by aspect
Lichen and moss favour shaded, damp aspects (north in the northern hemisphere), and water seeps leave dark vertical
stains below ledges and joints (desert varnish and "tintenstriche", the ink streaks of limestone walls: cyanobacteria
in seep lines). Inputs exist: sun direction / aspect, the sky occlusion map, WeatherMap3D wetness, and the rock face
streak block (6c23dd82a0). Stains follow the fall line below each ledge from the cliff relief offset field.

### Soil colour from parent rock
Bare soil inherits its parent material's colour (Munsell hues: red 2.5YR-5YR on iron-rich sandstone and basalt
saprolite, grey 10YR on granite grus, pale 10YR 7/x on limestone rendzina, dark 10YR 2/x where humus builds).
The generator's strata/geology gives rock type per cell; map it to a soil tint and mix by soil depth (thin soils
show more parent colour).

## Desert varnish on cliffs (26 Nov; Paulo picked "Yes, add varnish to cliffs")

Rock varnish (Dorn and Oberlander 1981; Dorn 2009, "Rock varnish and its use to study climatic change"; Liu and
Broecker 2000) is a manganese and iron oxide film micrometres thick, laid by dust and microbes over millennia on faces
that dry out between rare wettings. On desert walls it hangs in dark curtains from where runoff pours over a ledge or
the rim (the "tapestry" walls of the Colorado Plateau), over a thinner brown-grey patina on the whole old face; iron-rich
orange-brown varnish sits low on the face, in the band the talus and soil keep moist.

- **Amount:** `WorldContracts::varnish_for_precipitation(mm)` (none over 400 mm a year, full under 180 mm), the one
  rule the stones (RockField3D) and the cliffs share; GLSL `wc_varnish(p)` from the climate map, else the weather's
  season climate. Zero with no climate, so wet worlds are unchanged.
- **Shader (face weathering block, `tm_varnish`, 1 on):** a tint over the rock's own colour, never paint, so the
  texture and cracks show through (path tracing keeps the detail): patina x(0.62, 0.56, 0.5); curtains x(0.13, 0.11,
  0.1) as crisp drip stripes (tm_drip) 0.3 to 2.5 m wide from lips 30 m apart, running 12 to 45 m; iron x(0.85, 0.52,
  0.3) up to 1.5 to 4 m over the foot, fading out by 7 m. Lichen thins with it (it needs frequent wetting and its
  acids etch varnish). Shaded faces carry a third less curtain.
- **Foot:** Landscape3D publishes `terrain_cliff_foot_map` (R half float, over `terrain_cliff_rect`): for each wall
  texel the lowest ground down the heightfield's fall line until it eases under 40 degrees, up to 64 m out.
- **Gotcha:** `smoothstep(e0, e1, x)` with e0 > e1 is undefined in GLSL; lavapipe returned values outside 0..1 and the
  faces went black with neon red and blue specks. Write `1.0 - smoothstep(e1, e0, x)`.
- **Bench:** rocks bench `--world --world-size=1024 --view=cliff --cam=-16.4,97.5,8.4 --look=18,122,-14 --no-rocks
  --rain-climate=150`, and `--mat-prop=tm_varnish:0` for the before frame.
